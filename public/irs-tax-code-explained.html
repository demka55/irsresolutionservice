In one paragraph: The Internal Revenue Code (Title 26 of the U.S. Code) has 11 parts, but two of them run most Americans' money. Subtitle A decides how much income tax you owe: brackets, deductions, credits and retirement accounts. Subtitle F decides what happens next: deadlines, audits, penalties, interest, liens, levies, payment plans, settlements, and the 3-year and 10-year clocks that limit the IRS. Payroll taxes (Subtitle C) touch every paycheck. The rest barely affects the average person.

If you read nothing else: 7 rules that cover most of the tax code

  1. File, even if you can't pay. Not filing costs 10 times more in penalties than not paying (§ 6651).
  2. Open the first IRS letter. Every letter after it is more expensive. The last one is your bank account (§ 6331).
  3. Credits beat deductions. A $1,000 credit saves $1,000. A $1,000 deduction saves $100–$370, depending on your bracket.
  4. The IRS has clocks. Usually 3 years to audit you (§ 6501), 10 years to collect (§ 6502). Know your dates.
  5. Interest never sleeps. 7% a year, every day, even on a payment plan (§ 6621).
  6. Keep the receipt. No record, no deduction — that's how most audits are lost.
  7. The IRS can be wrong. You have the right to challenge it, see a manager and appeal (§ 7803).

Tax Court: $59,527 of unreported side-business income — but the penalty gets thrown out

Who: Rahman v. Commissioner (U.S. Tax Court bench opinion, Docket No. 8286-23) — a Pennsylvania couple

What happened: The husband ran several small businesses: tax preparation, insurance sales, a MoneyGram agency, cell-phone sales, and driving for a taxi company and Uber. The IRS added up every deposit in their bank accounts for 2019 — $81,378 — and subtracted the $21,850 they reported. The court agreed the $59,527 difference was taxable income. He said his records were destroyed when his store was looted, but the court wouldn't estimate expenses without other proof, so $2,340 of meals and $5,700 of repairs were thrown out. One win: the $2,557.80 accuracy penalty was cancelled because the IRS never showed a supervisor approved it in writing.

The part of the tax code it proves: § 61 (all income is taxable), § 162 (business expenses need proof), § 6751(b) (penalties need supervisor sign-off)
What it means for you

The IRS doesn't need your books to calculate your income — it can use your bank statements. And every penalty needs a manager's written approval first.

Romeo's take

Mixing business and personal money in one account is how the IRS turns every deposit into "income." Open a separate business account — it's free and it's the best audit protection there is. And always ask for the penalty approval form. Here it saved them $2,557.80.

This is what most people mean by "the tax code." It decides what counts as income, what you can subtract, what rate you pay and which credits you get. Below are the parts regular people actually use, grouped by situation.

Your paycheck and the basics

How much of my income goes to tax? (the brackets)

§ 1

Your income is taxed in layers. The first chunk is taxed at 10%, the next at 12%, then 22%, and so on up to 37%. Moving into a higher bracket only affects the dollars above the line — never the ones below it.

Real example

Maria is a teacher earning $68,350 in 2026. After the $16,100 standard deduction, $52,250 is taxable. Her federal tax is about $6,207 — roughly 9% of her salary. She's "in the 22% bracket," but only $1,850 of her income is actually taxed at 22%.

Romeo's take

The most expensive tax myth I hear is "I don't want a raise, it'll push me into a higher bracket." It won't cost you a dime on the money you already make. Take the raise.

Is this money taxable? (Almost always, yes)

§ 61

The law starts from one rule: all income is taxable unless a specific section says it isn't. Cash jobs, side gigs, app payments for work, crypto gains, prizes and gambling winnings all count. Gifts, inheritances, life insurance payouts and most home-sale profit are the big exceptions.

Real example

Jake delivers food on weekends and makes $8,000. He never gets a tax form for it. It's still taxable — the IRS doesn't need a form to count it, and the app may report it anyway.

Romeo's take

"They didn't send me a 1099" is not a defense — it's an audit waiting to happen. If money came in because you did work, report it. The IRS gets most of the same data you do.

The standard deduction — the tax-free chunk everyone gets

§ 63

Before tax is figured, you get to subtract a flat amount: $16,100 if single, $32,200 if married filing jointly, $24,150 for head of household (2026). You only itemize if your deductions add up to more.

Real example

Dan and Priya have $9,000 of mortgage interest, $6,000 of property tax, $5,000 of state tax and $3,000 of donations — $23,000 total. The standard deduction is $32,200, so they take that instead and get a bigger break without keeping a single receipt for it.

Romeo's take

About nine out of ten people take the standard deduction now. If you're still shoeboxing receipts for itemizing, run the numbers — you may be doing homework for nothing.

Credits for kids and working families

§ 24 · § 32

A credit comes straight off your tax bill, dollar for dollar — worth more than a deduction. The Child Tax Credit is $2,200 per child for 2026. The Earned Income Credit is paid even if you owe no tax at all, up to $8,231 for a family with three or more kids.

Real example

A married couple with two kids who owes $5,000 in tax before credits gets $4,400 knocked off by the Child Tax Credit and owes $600. A single dad earning $28,000 with two kids may get several thousand dollars back from the Earned Income Credit on top of his withholding.

Romeo's take

The Earned Income Credit is one of the most-audited lines on an individual return. If you claim it, keep proof the kids lived with you — school records, a lease, a doctor's letter. That one folder ends most of those audits.

IRC Subtitle B · §§ 2001 – 2801

Tax on money you leave or give away

Officially: Estate and Gift Taxes · Read the code ↗
How much it touches everyday people:
Who it affects: Very few people — only estates and lifetime gifts above $15 million per person.

Almost no one pays these taxes anymore, because the tax-free amount is $15 million per person. What matters for normal families is the paperwork and one big break for heirs.

What families actually need to know

The $15 million rule — why your family almost certainly won't owe estate tax

§ 2010

Each person can leave or give away up to $15 million (2026) with no federal estate or gift tax. Married couples get $30 million. Anything left to a spouse is tax-free no matter the amount.

Real example

When Ruth passes away, she leaves her $900,000 house and $600,000 401(k) to her two kids. Total: $1.5 million. Federal estate tax: $0. (Some states have their own estate tax with lower limits — check yours.)

Romeo's take

Estate tax scares people who will never owe it. Spend that energy on a will and up-to-date beneficiary forms instead — that's where families actually lose money and sleep.

Giving money to family

§ 2503(b)

You can give up to $19,000 a year (2026) to as many people as you want with no paperwork. Give more and you file a gift tax return (Form 709), but you almost certainly owe nothing — it just counts against your $15 million.

Real example

Grandma gives her grandson $25,000 toward a house. She files Form 709 showing $6,000 over the limit. She owes $0, and he owes no tax on the gift either.

Romeo's take

The person receiving a gift never owes income tax on it. Paying a grandkid's tuition or medical bills directly to the school or hospital doesn't even count toward the $19,000.

Inherited property: the "step-up" that wipes out the gain

§ 1014

When you inherit property, its tax value resets to what it was worth when the owner died. The growth during their lifetime is never taxed.

Real example

Your parents bought their house for $100,000. It's worth $500,000 when you inherit it. You sell it for $500,000 and owe $0 in capital-gains tax. If they had given it to you while alive, you'd have taken their $100,000 basis — and owed tax on $400,000.

Romeo's take

This is why "just put the house in the kids' names now" is often terrible tax advice. Gifting during life can cost your kids tens of thousands. Ask before you sign a deed.

Filing

Do I have to file?

§ 6012

Generally yes if your income is above the standard deduction — and always if you made $400 or more of self-employment profit, even if you owe nothing else.

Real example

A college student makes $3,000 at a summer job and $500 of profit selling on Etsy. The $3,000 alone wouldn't require a return — the $500 of business profit does.

Common questions

What is the IRS tax code, in simple terms?
It's the federal tax law Congress wrote — Title 26 of the U.S. Code. It's split into 11 parts ("subtitles"). Two of them run most people's tax lives: Subtitle A decides how much income tax you owe, and Subtitle F decides how the IRS audits, penalizes and collects. The IRS enforces the code; it doesn't write it.
Which parts of the tax code matter most to regular people?
Subtitle A (income tax: brackets, deductions, credits, retirement accounts) and Subtitle F (filing, audits, penalties, interest, liens, levies, payment plans and settlements). Subtitle C (payroll taxes) explains your pay stub. Most of the rest applies to specific industries or very large estates.
How long can the IRS come after me for a tax debt?
Usually 10 years from the date the tax was assessed (§ 6502). Some events, such as a settlement offer, a hearing request or bankruptcy, pause the clock. When it runs out, the debt and the lien are gone automatically.
How far back can the IRS audit me?
Usually 3 years after you file (§ 6501), 6 years if you left out more than 25% of your income, and with no limit if you never filed or committed fraud.
What happens if I don't file my taxes?
The IRS can file a return for you using the income it knows about, usually without your deductions or credits (§ 6020(b)). Penalties are much bigger for not filing than for not paying, and eventually come liens and levies. You can replace the IRS's version by filing your own, which is almost always cheaper.
Can I still lower last year's taxes with a retirement plan?
If you're self-employed or own a business, often yes. You can fund a SEP-IRA or profit-sharing plan for a tax year up to your filing deadline, including extensions — and since the SECURE Act you can even set up a new profit-sharing plan after year-end. The 2026 limit is $72,000 per person, generally capped at 25% of pay.
Is not paying my taxes a crime?
Being unable to pay is not a crime. Willful tax evasion (§ 7201) and willfully not filing (§ 7203) are. Honest mistakes and unpaid balances are handled with penalties, interest and collection.
What tax cases were in the news in October 2026?
Examples include: the U.S. Tax Court's October 7, 2026 Rahman decision, which upheld $59,527 of unreported side-business income found through bank deposits but threw out the penalty for lack of supervisor approval; the IRS's $1.57 million lien against Todd and Julie Chrisley, reported October 15; automatic adoption-credit refunds to more than 50,000 families announced October 16; and prison sentences on October 21 and 29 for business owners in Kansas and Texas who kept employees' withheld payroll taxes.
Can the IRS be wrong?
Yes. IRS employees use the same code and manual everyone else does, and they make mistakes. You have the right to challenge the IRS, ask for a manager and appeal (§ 7803(a)(3)).
