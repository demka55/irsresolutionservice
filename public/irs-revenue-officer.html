IRS Revenue Officer

Romeo has been quoted on tax matters by U.S. News, Kiplinger, NBC/CNBC (twice), and Realtor.com, and writes for Intuit's Tax Pro Center. More about Romeo →

An IRS Revenue Officer has been assigned to your case. Here is exactly what that means — from a former IRS Tax Examiner who now represents taxpayers in front of them.

The bottom line: A Revenue Officer (RO) is a field-based IRS employee with the authority to levy bank accounts, garnish wages, seize assets, and file tax liens on the same day they decide to act. Unlike notices from the Automated Collection System, a Revenue Officer is a human being with discretion — which means how you handle the first contact matters enormously. Do not meet with one alone.

Since 2023, most first contact comes as a scheduled appointment letter (Letter 725-B), not a surprise knock. A real Revenue Officer carries two IRS credentials (a pocket commission and an HSPD-12 card) and will only ever ask you to pay the U.S. Treasury.

Why a Revenue Officer is different from every other IRS contact

Most IRS collection happens through the Automated Collection System (ACS) — a call center that sends notices and processes requests. ACS can levy, but it typically goes through a series of notices first and gives you time to respond.

A Revenue Officer is different in three important ways:

  • They meet you in person. Since August 2023 the IRS has ended most unannounced Revenue Officer visits: an RO now typically mails an appointment letter (Letter 725-B) and schedules a face-to-face meeting at your home, business or an IRS office. Unannounced visits still happen in limited cases, such as serving a summons or seizing assets at risk of disappearing. Either way, this is not a phone call you can screen.
  • They have immediate enforcement authority. An RO can issue a levy the same day, with no additional notice required, once your right to a Collection Due Process (CDP) hearing has been triggered by a prior LT11 notice.
  • They are a human with discretion. An RO who believes you are cooperating and working toward resolution will typically give you time. An RO who believes you are hiding assets or stalling will move fast.

Revenue Officer cases are reserved for the most serious collection situations: large balances, unfiled returns, payroll tax delinquencies, or cases where the Automated Collection System has been unable to collect. If you have an RO, the IRS has decided your case warrants individual human attention.

Revenue Officer vs. Revenue Agent — which one do you have?

The titles sound alike, but the jobs are different, and so is your strategy.

Revenue Officer (Collection)Revenue Agent (Examination)
JobCollect tax that's already owed and get missing returns filedAudit returns to decide whether more tax is owed
Shows up whenA balance or unfiled returns went unresolved through mailed noticesYour return was selected for a field audit
Main toolsLevies, liens, seizures, Form 433-A/433-B financial statements, Form 9297 deadlinesDocument requests, interviews, proposed adjustments
The question you're negotiatingHow the debt gets paidWhether the debt exists

The two arms don't share a case file in practice: once an audit closes, the balance moves to Collection, and the Revenue Officer has no relationship with the examiner who created it.

How to tell a real Revenue Officer from a scammer

Impersonators exploit exactly the fear an RO visit creates. A real Revenue Officer:

  • Usually writes first. You'll normally have received several mailed notices — and, since 2023, typically a Letter 725-B scheduling the meeting.
  • Carries two credentials: a pocket commission and an HSPD-12 card. You have the right to see both. If you ask to verify the HSPD-12 card, the officer will give you a dedicated IRS number to confirm their identity.
  • Only takes payment to the U.S. Treasury. Never a gift card, prepaid debit card, wire to a third party, or crypto.
  • Doesn't threaten arrest, deportation, or loss of a license.

If anything is off, don't pay. Check your balance in your IRS Online Account, and report impersonators to TIGTA at 800-366-4484.

What's changed in IRS field collection in 2025–2026

  • Collection over audits. The IRS has publicly shifted resources toward collection, analytics and automation. Bloomberg Tax reported in December 2025 that Revenue Officers are increasingly reserved for the hardest cases: business employment taxes, potential fraud, and high-wealth taxpayers with large balances or unfiled returns.
  • Tougher first interviews (practitioner reports). Tax practitioners report that since mid-2026 Revenue Officers have been asking for full payment at the initial interview and looking at assets — home equity, investments, retirement accounts, digital assets — before agreeing to a payment plan or Currently Not Collectible status. Expect a detailed financial interview and come with your Form 433-A complete.
  • Narrower Tax Court review in some CDP cases. In Zuch v. Commissioner (June 2025), the Supreme Court held that the Tax Court loses jurisdiction over a Collection Due Process case once the IRS no longer intends to levy — for example, because the balance was satisfied. Raise liability disputes early and through the right channel.
  • Unfiled returns after October 15. The extended deadline for 2025 individual returns passed on October 15, 2026. Unfiled returns are one of the most common reasons a case ends up with a Revenue Officer, and an RO generally won't agree to any payment arrangement until every required return is filed.

What an IRS Revenue Officer can do — and what they cannot

Can do — same day

Issue wage levy (Form 668-W) to employer. Issue bank levy (Form 668-A). File Notice of Federal Tax Lien. Seize physical business assets. Request full financial disclosure.

Can do — with time

Recommend criminal referral for egregious cases. Pursue personal liability of business owners for trust fund taxes. Contact your clients, customers, or tenants directly.

Cannot do

Enter your home without permission or a warrant. Seize assets exempt from levy (certain retirement funds, some personal property). Arrest you. Make threats or demands outside IRS guidelines.

Must do

Show both credentials (pocket commission and HSPD-12 card) and provide Publication 1 (Your Rights as a Taxpayer). Suspend the interview if you ask to consult a representative. Respect the CDP hearing process if it was properly initiated.

The most important thing to do when an RO makes contact

You have the right to representation. When an IRS Revenue Officer makes contact — whether by showing up at your door, calling you, or leaving a business card — you are not required to discuss your case without a qualified representative present.

The correct response is polite and specific: "I want to cooperate fully with the IRS, and I'm retaining a representative. I'll have them contact you within [48 hours / this week]. Can I have your direct contact information?"

Write down their name, badge number, and the phone number on the card they leave. Call a CPA or Enrolled Agent immediately.

Do not voluntarily provide financial information, invite them inside, or make statements about your income, assets, or whereabouts of funds without representation present. Anything you say will be documented and used to determine the resolution path — or enforcement action.

Why the first meeting matters so much

Revenue Officers are trained to assess cooperation and financial disclosure simultaneously. If you appear cooperative and provide credible information, they are much more likely to work toward a voluntary resolution: an installment agreement, an OIC, or a CNC determination. If you appear evasive, they are more likely to pursue immediate enforcement.

A good representative can frame your situation accurately — showing the RO exactly what you have, exactly what you can pay, and exactly what resolution path makes sense — in a way that satisfies their file requirements without giving them grounds for aggressive action.

Large balances still get payment plans

A Revenue Officer assignment usually means a big balance, and people assume that rules out a payment plan. It doesn't. Balances over $50,000 require full financial disclosure (Form 433-A, or 433-B for a business) and the RO's approval, but they get approved every week when the numbers are presented properly.

Romeo Razi, CPA — Missed Deadline Video

"I once had a client that owed $440,000 to the IRS. I got them on a payment plan. If I could get that person on a payment plan, you could get on a payment plan with $100,000 or $120,000. If you owe over $50,000, sometimes people will make a quick payment to get under $50,000 so they can just streamline it online."

IRS staffing and Revenue Officers in Las Vegas

Revenue Officer availability varies dramatically by location. Las Vegas is a particularly striking example of how IRS budget cuts have affected enforcement capacity. As of 2024-2025, the Las Vegas IRS field office went from 20+ Revenue Officers to fewer than five.

What this means in practice: if you have a balance in the Las Vegas metro area and no RO has been assigned yet, the probability of one being assigned has dropped significantly. Cases are sitting longer in ACS. The IRS simply doesn't have the staffing to field-assign every case it would normally assign.

This does not mean enforcement has stopped — ACS levies still happen automatically. But the hands-on, discretionary enforcement that a Revenue Officer represents has been substantially reduced in high-vacancy field offices.

Romeo Razi, CPA — Former IRS Tax Examiner

"When I worked in Vegas, we had a full office of Revenue Officers. Cases moved. Now, with fewer than five officers covering the entire metro area, cases are sitting. I still recommend that clients respond proactively — the IRS can always reassign or escalate — but the practical reality is that enforcement timelines have stretched. The collection statute keeps running the whole time."

Payroll tax delinquencies — the highest-priority RO cases

If your RO case involves payroll taxes (trust fund taxes — the employee's share of FICA and income tax withholding), the situation is more serious than a personal income tax balance. Payroll tax cases are among the highest priorities in the IRS field collection program for two reasons:

  • The IRS takes it personally. Trust fund taxes were collected from employees. Not remitting them is viewed as the company using those funds for its own purposes. Revenue Officers pursue these aggressively.
  • The Trust Fund Recovery Penalty (TFRP). The IRS can assess the full amount of unpaid trust fund taxes personally against any "responsible person" — not just the business entity. Owners, officers, bookkeepers, and anyone who had signing authority can be held personally liable.

If an RO is investigating a payroll tax situation, get representation immediately. The TFRP assessment process has specific procedural requirements — including a required manager signature — and procedural errors during the assessment process can be challenged.

When a Revenue Officer comes to your business — what can they do?

A Revenue Officer visiting your business is a more complex situation than a home visit. At a business, the RO has broader immediate access and can observe your operations, inventory, equipment, and accounts receivable in a way that informs their collection strategy.

What an RO can do at your business premises

  • Enter publicly accessible areas — the front office, reception area, retail floor. They cannot enter private offices, warehouses, or restricted areas without permission.
  • Speak with employees — they can ask employees basic identifying questions. Employees don't have to answer questions about company finances.
  • Observe visible assets — equipment, inventory, and vehicles visible from accessible areas can inform asset seizure decisions.
  • Serve a summons on the business — requiring production of books, records, and financial information under IRC § 7602.
  • Contact your customers and accounts receivable — using Form 668-A, the IRS can levy amounts owed to your business by your customers. This is called an "accounts receivable levy" and can devastate cash flow overnight.

The accounts receivable levy — the RO's most powerful business tool

Most business owners don't realize that the IRS can levy money that other people owe them. Under IRC § 6331, the levy attaches to "all property and rights to property." That includes your accounts receivable — amounts your customers owe you for work already completed.

The IRS sends Form 668-A directly to your customers, instructing them to pay the IRS instead of you. Your customer is then legally required to pay the IRS. If they don't, they become personally liable. This levy:

  • Can be served on multiple customers simultaneously
  • Covers all amounts currently owed (not just amounts due in the next 30 days)
  • Can seriously damage customer relationships
  • Is particularly devastating for service businesses with recurring receivables

Payroll tax cases — why they escalate faster

If the Revenue Officer visit relates to unpaid payroll taxes (Form 941 delinquencies), the timeline for aggressive action is much shorter. The IRS treats trust fund taxes — employee income tax and FICA withheld from paychecks but not remitted — as theft from employees. ROs handling payroll cases are specifically trained to move quickly and are less likely to extend timelines for voluntary resolution.

In payroll cases, the RO will typically:

  • Conduct a trust fund interview to determine personal liability (Form 4180)
  • Identify all "responsible persons" — owners, officers, bookkeepers, check signers
  • Prepare Trust Fund Recovery Penalty (TFRP) assessments against each responsible person individually
  • Require the business to set up payroll tax deposits going forward as a condition of any resolution

The IRS collections system — what most taxpayers don't know

The IRS has two completely separate arms: Examination (audit) and Collection. Once an auditor makes an assessment and closes a case, it moves entirely to Collection — different people, different rulebook, different strategies. A Revenue Officer in collections has no relationship with the examiner who created the assessment.

TIGTA — the IRS's internal affairs and how to use it

The Treasury Inspector General for Tax Administration (TIGTA) is the internal affairs unit of the IRS. Revenue Officers answer to their managers — but their managers answer to TIGTA. If a Revenue Officer is behaving inappropriately, threatening, or wasting government resources, TIGTA is the escalation point that managers take seriously.

Romeo Razi, CPA — Former IRS Tax Examiner

"I had a collections officer who was coming at my client very aggressively and inappropriately. When her paperwork arrived at my office, I called her manager and said: 'Your employee is stealing from the American taxpayer. She drove 40 minutes each way to hand-deliver something she could have faxed, then logged two hours on this case she didn't work. I can contact TIGTA and have them investigate her conduct — and I don't think I'm the only taxpayer she's treated this way.' After that call, the officer completely stood down. I knew her system better than she thought I did."

TIGTA complaints are not something to throw around lightly — but when the conduct is genuine and documented, contacting the manager with specific, documented allegations (time logs, wasted travel, inappropriate communications) is a legitimate and effective tool. If an IRS agent has TIGTA investigate them, their professional life is disrupted for two to three years.

The union dynamic — why manager conversations matter

When Romeo escalated the worker reclassification case described above to the manager level, the manager started talking about internal politics: IRS agents have a union. Reprimands are difficult. Cases get stuck because of personnel issues that have nothing to do with the taxpayer.

Understanding this changes how you negotiate with managers. You're not just arguing the merits of the case — you're giving the manager a path to close the matter without a difficult internal confrontation. Framing it as "if this goes to Appeals, you'll have no grounds and I'll need to charge my client another $1,000 for the process" is more effective than pure legal argument. The manager wants the case resolved. Help them resolve it.

Frequently asked questions about IRS Revenue Officers

Can I refuse to let a Revenue Officer into my home?
Yes. A Revenue Officer cannot enter your home without your permission or a court order. You can speak with them at the door, take their information, and tell them your representative will follow up. They cannot compel entry into a private residence.
How long does a Revenue Officer case typically take?
It depends heavily on complexity and cooperation. Simple cases where you provide financial information quickly and agree to an installment agreement or CNC can close in 30-60 days. Complex cases involving multiple unfiled returns, large balances, or business assets can take six months to a year or more. Cases that escalate to enforcement action (levy, seizure) can move much faster once that decision is made.
Will the RO recommend criminal charges?
Criminal referrals are rare and reserved for the most egregious situations — deliberate evasion, fraud, or failure to remit payroll taxes when there's clear ability to pay. Most Revenue Officer cases end with civil collection: a payment arrangement, an OIC, or a CNC determination. Cooperation and accuracy dramatically reduce any criminal exposure.
What happens if I ignore a Revenue Officer?
Ignoring an RO is one of the worst possible responses. An RO who cannot make contact will document attempts, then proceed to the most aggressive enforcement option available. This typically means levying whatever bank accounts and income sources the IRS has on file and filing a lien against all property. Revenue Officers have authority to act without additional advance notice once the CDP process has been completed.
What does an IRS Revenue Officer do?
A Revenue Officer is an IRS collection employee who handles serious cases in person: large balances, unfiled returns, and payroll tax debts. They gather financial information, set deadlines, arrange payment plans or Currently Not Collectible status, and, when needed, file liens, issue levies and seize assets.
Will a Revenue Officer show up at my house without warning?
Usually not anymore. Since August 2023 the IRS has ended most unannounced Revenue Officer visits and instead mails an appointment letter, Letter 725-B. Unannounced visits are now limited to situations such as serving a summons or seizing assets that could be moved out of reach.
How do I know a Revenue Officer is real?
A real Revenue Officer carries two credentials, a pocket commission and an HSPD-12 card, and you can ask to see both. They will give you an IRS number to verify the HSPD-12 card, and they will only ask you to pay the U.S. Treasury, never by gift card, prepaid card or crypto. Report impersonators to TIGTA at 800-366-4484.
What is the $75 rule in the IRS?
The $75 rule is an audit substantiation rule, not a collection rule: for most business travel, gift and transportation expenses under $75 (lodging excepted), you don't need a receipt, though you still need a record of the amount, date, place and business purpose. Revenue Officers reviewing your finances use the IRS Collection Financial Standards instead.
How much do IRS Revenue Officers make?
IRS job postings for Internal Revenue Officer have listed starting pay around $75,055 a year at the IR-7 grade, with higher pay at senior grades and in high-cost locations.
