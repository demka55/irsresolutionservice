    What "reasonable salary" actually means

    If you own an S corp and work in it, the law treats you as an employee. You have to pay yourself a W-2 salary — with Social Security and Medicare taken out — before you take the rest out as distributions, which skip those taxes. That's the whole S corp tax break, and it's why the IRS watches the salary number so closely.

    There's no percentage in the tax code. The test is simple to say and hard to fake: what would you have to pay someone else to do your job? The IRS lists what courts look at (Fact Sheet FS-2008-25):

    Your training and experienceA 20-year CPA isn't worth an entry-level wage.
    Your dutiesOwner, salesperson and manager all at once counts for more.
    Your hoursFull-time work rarely supports a part-time salary.
    What similar businesses payJob ads and wage surveys for your role and area.
    What you pay your employeesPaying staff more than yourself is a red flag.
    Your dividend historyBig distributions next to a small salary stand out.
    How bonuses are paidTiming and method of pay to key people.
    Formulas and agreementsWritten compensation agreements help.
    RR
    Romeo's take

    The "60/40 rule" you'll see online isn't in any law or IRS guidance. I never once used a percentage in an audit. I asked what the job was worth. Write down how you got your number — job ads, a wage survey, what you'd pay a manager — and keep it in your files. That one page is your defense.

    8th Circuit Court of Appeals · February 21, 2012

    A CPA paid himself $24,000. The court said $91,044.

    David E. Watson, P.C. v. United States. An experienced CPA working full time at a firm with close to $3 million in revenue paid himself a $24,000 salary in 2002 and 2003 while taking $203,651 and $175,470 in distributions. The government's expert said a reasonable salary was $91,044. The court agreed, and the extra $67,044 a year became subject to Social Security and Medicare tax, plus penalties and interest.

    Source: Journal of Accountancy, Feb. 2012

    U.S. Tax Court · 2013

    A real estate broker paid $0 and took $240,000 out. The court said $83,200.

    Sean McAlary Ltd. v. Commissioner (T.C. Summary Opinion 2013-62). The owner ran the whole brokerage and was its top salesperson. His salary was set at $24,000 but never actually paid, and he took $240,000 in distributions. The IRS's expert argued for about $100,000. The court set it at $83,200 and assessed payroll tax on that amount.

    Source: Iowa State Center for Agricultural Law and Taxation

    U.S. Tax Court · August 7, 2013

    No salary, a small profit — and still $30,000 a year of wages.

    Glass Blocks Unlimited v. Commissioner (T.C. Memo. 2013-180). The president was the company's only full-time worker and made every decision. He took no salary; money moved to him as distributions and undocumented “loans.” The court treated more than $30,000 in each of 2007 and 2008 as wages — even though the company's taxable income was well below that — and upheld failure-to-file and failure-to-deposit penalties.

    Source: Iowa State Center for Agricultural Law and Taxation

    U.S. Tax Court · December 2016

    A financial advisor paid himself $35,000 a year. It didn't matter.

    Fleischer v. Commissioner (T.C. Memo. 2016-238). Ryan Fleischer, an independent broker and insurance agent, paid himself about $35,000 a year from his S corp and took $274,893 out as distributions over 2009–2011. The problem: his brokerage and insurance contracts were in his own name, not the corporation's. The court said the commissions were his personal self-employment income and upheld a $41,563 tax bill. If you're licensed personally — broker, insurance agent, some advisors — the contract has to be with your S corp.

    Source: Kitces.com

    7th Circuit Court of Appeals · 1990

    A lawyer paid himself nothing and called it dividends.

    Joseph Radtke, S.C. v. United States, 895 F.2d 1196. Radtke was his law firm's only full-time lawyer. In 1982 he took no salary and paid himself $18,225 as dividends. The court held that all of it was wages subject to payroll tax, because it was really payment for his legal work.

    Source: Justia (full opinion)

    9th Circuit Court of Appeals · 1990

    An accountant said he “donated” his work to his own firm.

    Spicer Accounting, Inc. v. United States, 918 F.2d 90. The firm's only accountant took dividends instead of a salary and argued he was volunteering his services. The court said what matters is whether the money was paid for work, not what it's called — and treated it as wages.

    Source: Leagle (full opinion)

    RR
    Romeo's take

    Look at the dates — 1990, 2012, 2013, 2016. The rule hasn't changed in 35 years, and the IRS keeps winning with the same argument. None of these owners kept their low number, and every one of them paid lawyers or experts to fight about it. Writing down a defensible salary up front costs a fraction of that.

    The opposite problem exists too: in Clary Hood, Inc. v. Commissioner (T.C. Memo. 2022-15, aff'd 4th Cir. 2023) a C corporation's $5 million bonus to its CEO was partly disallowed as too high. That's rarely an issue for S corps, where the IRS's concern is salaries that are too low. The Tax Adviser, Oct. 2024

    Romeo's take

    Fewer IRS agents doesn't mean nobody's looking. A $0 salary next to big distributions shows up on the face of the return — no agent has to dig for it. And the two October sentences are a different, worse problem: once you withhold tax from a paycheck, that money isn't yours. Pay yourself on payroll, and send in every dollar you withhold.

    Once you set the salary: run payroll like the IRS is watching

    An S corp salary has to go through real payroll: withholding, quarterly Form 941, a W-2 in January. The taxes withheld from your own paycheck are "trust fund" money — if the business doesn't send them in, the IRS can bill you personally (§ 6672). And if you get a penalty notice, the IRS has to follow its own rules too.

    Common questions

    What is reasonable compensation for an S corporation owner?
    It's the salary you'd have to pay someone else to do the work you do for your business. The IRS requires S corporation owners who work in the business to pay themselves a reasonable W-2 salary before taking profits out as distributions. Courts look at your training and experience, duties, hours, what comparable businesses pay, and what non-owner employees earn (IRS FS-2008-25).
    Is there a 60/40 rule for S corp salary?
    No. The "60/40" (60% salary, 40% distributions) split is a rule of thumb, not anything in the tax code or IRS guidance. The IRS standard is what your services are worth on the market. A 60/40 split can be too high for one business and too low for another.
    Can an S corp owner pay themselves no salary?
    Only if they don't work in the business or the business has no profit to pay them with. If you perform services and take distributions, the IRS can reclassify distributions as wages and assess back payroll taxes, penalties and interest — as it did in Sean McAlary Ltd. v. Commissioner (T.C. Summary Opinion 2013-62), where a real-estate broker took no salary and $240,000 in distributions, and the court set her salary at $83,200.
    How much can I save with an S corporation?
    The savings come from avoiding the 15.3% Social Security and Medicare tax on the profit you take as distributions instead of salary. They're reduced by a smaller 20% QBI deduction, a payroll service and a separate corporate tax return. For many owners the S corp starts to pay off somewhere around $50,000–$80,000 of profit, but it depends on what a reasonable salary is for your work — use the calculator above.
    Is the IRS still checking S corp salaries in 2026?
    Yes, though with fewer people. TIGTA reported on August 26, 2026 that IRS examination and collection staff fell about 27% between fiscal 2024 and 2025, and the Inflation Reduction Act enforcement money ran out on December 31, 2025. But the low-salary issue is an easy one for the IRS to spot from the return itself: in a 2021 report, TIGTA found 266,095 single-owner S corporations with more than $100,000 of profit that reported $0 officer pay while taking $69 billion in distributions.
    Is there a salary-to-distribution ratio that's safe?
    No. In Fleischer v. Commissioner (T.C. Memo. 2016-238) a financial advisor paid himself about $35,000 a year — roughly 38% of what he took out — and still lost, because his commissions were paid to him personally rather than to his S corporation. Courts look at what your work is worth and how the income was earned, not at a ratio.
    What happens if the IRS says my S corp salary is too low?
    The IRS can recharacterize part of your distributions as wages and assess the employer and employee Social Security and Medicare tax on them, plus penalties and interest. In David E. Watson, P.C. v. United States (8th Cir. 2012), a CPA who paid himself $24,000 a year was found to owe payroll tax on a $91,044 salary.
