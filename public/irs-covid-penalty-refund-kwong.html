IRS COVID penalty refund after Kwong v. US: where things stand, and who can still file

Can you still get an IRS COVID penalty refund? Possibly. A November 25, 2025 court decision (Kwong v. United States) read the tax code to say that filing and payment deadlines were automatically postponed for the entire COVID federal disaster period — January 20, 2020 through July 10, 2023. If that reading holds, late-filing and late-payment penalties (and related interest) for that 3.5-year window should never have been charged. As of October 2026 the ruling is on appeal at the Federal Circuit, the IRS is denying some of the 275,000+ claims it has received. The July 10, 2026 date has passed, but if you paid COVID-era penalties or interest in the last two years, you can generally still file Form 843 for those payments.

What Kwong v. US said, and why it could mean an IRS COVID penalty refund

The case turns on one provision: IRC § 7508A(d), the "mandatory postponement" rule Congress added for federally declared disasters. As it existed when COVID was declared, it automatically postpones filing and payment deadlines for the period a federal disaster declaration is in effect, plus 60 days.

Nobody expected a disaster declaration to last 3.5 years. But COVID's did: January 20, 2020 through May 11, 2023. Add 60 days and you land on July 10, 2023.

In Kwong v. United States, 179 Fed. Cl. 382 (Nov. 25, 2025), the Court of Federal Claims held that "the plain meaning of that statute is that the automatic extension runs from the beginning of the disaster declaration, through the end of the declared disaster period, and until 60 days after the end of the declared disaster period." Under that logic, returns and payments due anywhere inside that window were not legally late until after July 10, 2023 — which means the IRS should not have assessed failure-to-file, failure-to-pay, or estimated-tax penalties for that period, nor charged interest on those amounts.

Important — The Law Is Not Settled

The government disagrees with this reading and appealed to the Federal Circuit in May 2026. In its September 2026 brief it argues the automatic postponement lasted only 60 days, ending March 20, 2020. Final resolution could take years and could reach the Supreme Court. Filing a claim does not guarantee a refund. It preserves your right to one if the taxpayer-favorable reading holds. If you never file and the decision is upheld, the statute of limitations will generally bar you from ever collecting. That asymmetry (small cost to file, total loss if you don't) is why the National Taxpayer Advocate publicly urged taxpayers to act.

Latest developments (updated October 29, 2026)

A lot has happened since the decision came down. Here is each development in order, what it means, and Romeo's take on it.

December 2025 — Congress fixes the “lookback” trap (P.L. 119-64)

The Disaster Related Extension of Deadlines Act added IRC § 7508A(f), effective for refund claims filed after December 26, 2025. Normally a refund is capped at what you paid in the 3 years (plus extensions) before your claim, so a timely claim could still recover little or nothing. The new rule extends that lookback by the disaster postponement. Without it, many Kwong claims would have been timely but empty.

May 15, 2026 — The government appeals

The Department of Justice filed a notice of appeal, docketed at the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit as Kwong v. United States, No. 26-1843. On May 20, 2026 the IRS also issued an Action on Decision on the related Tax Court case, Abdo v. Commissioner, 162 T.C. 148 (2024). It says the IRS will not accept any reading of the automatic postponement longer than 60 days. IRS Appeals has been told to grant only that 60-day relief while the appeal is pending.

"This was always going to be appealed. There's too much money on the line for the government to let one trial-level ruling stand. That's exactly why you file now: the appeal decides whether you get paid, but only people with a claim on file are in line when it does."

July 1, 2026 — The IRS opens online filing for Kwong claims

The IRS posted special filing instructions for Kwong claims. Individuals with an IRS Online Account can now file Form 843 electronically, but only for fully paid penalties and interest. Businesses, and individuals who prefer paper, write “Kwong vs. United States” across the top and mail the form to Internal Revenue Service, 1973 N Rulon White Blvd., Ogden, UT 84201. Tax professionals cannot use the online tool for clients.

July 10, 2026 — The general deadline passes

July 10, 2026 (three years after the end of the postponement period) was the date the National Taxpayer Advocate urged most taxpayers to meet. It was never the only deadline. Claims tied to payments made in the last two years can still be timely; see below.

August 2026 — Other courts wait for the appeal

Related cases are piling up behind Kwong. In BMW (US) Holding Corp. v. United States (Fed. Cl., Aug. 5, 2026), the court put the government's motion to dismiss on hold until the Federal Circuit rules. Western Digital Corp. v. United States (Fed. Cl.) seeks about $20.7 million of COVID-period interest. In Wepplo v. Commissioner, the Tax Court invited outside briefs on how the postponement affects interest. Other cases have failed on procedure: courts refused to let taxpayers raise the Kwong argument too late in a criminal case and a collection due process case.

September 16, 2026 — The government's opening brief

In its Federal Circuit brief, the government argues the lower court used FEMA's “incident period” when the statute says “incident date,” so the automatic postponement ran only 60 days from January 20, 2020, ending March 20, 2020. As fallbacks, it argues for May 21, 2020, or a one-year maximum. It warns that the lower court's reading could affect “tens of millions of taxpayers” and cost “tens of billions of dollars.” Mr. Kwong's response brief and oral argument come next. A decision is unlikely before 2027.

October 2026 — IRS denials begin, and the Taxpayer Advocate pushes back

The IRS has received more than 275,000 Kwong claims on Form 843, still a small fraction of the tens of millions of taxpayers who may be affected. Some filers are now getting denial letters or “no consideration” letters. In mid-October, National Taxpayer Advocate Erin Collins asked the IRS to stop issuing denials, hold claims until the courts resolve the issue (as the Internal Revenue Manual generally allows for protective claims), review the letters already sent, and fix its screening criteria. “Taxpayers who took timely steps to preserve their rights should not be placed at risk of losing those rights,” she wrote.

Got a denial or “no consideration” letter? Do this

1. Keep it and note the date. A formal notice of claim disallowance generally starts a two-year deadline to file a refund suit (IRC § 6532). Continuing to talk with the IRS does not stop that clock.
2. Check which letter it is. A request to abate penalties you haven't paid yet is commonly answered with Letter 916C (“no consideration”) and is not held as a protective claim. Once you pay, a new refund claim for that payment can be filed.
3. Don't refile on the same facts by reflex. Get the account transcript first and see what was actually paid, and when.
4. Watch for the IRS's response to the Taxpayer Advocate. If the IRS agrees to hold claims, some denials may be revisited.

Step 1: Find out what you actually paid — pull your transcripts

Before filing anything, know your numbers. Your IRS account transcripts for tax years 2019 through 2022 show every penalty and interest charge, with dates. You can pull them free in minutes through your IRS Online Account.

What to look for on the transcript:

  • TC 166 / 276 — late-filing and late-payment penalty assessments (and their dollar amounts)
  • TC 196 — interest assessed
  • Payment dates — these determine whether your claim window is still open (more on that below)

If you have multiple years, business accounts, or a Revenue Officer involved, a tax professional with a power of attorney can pull every year and every module at once and total the exposure in one pass.

Step 2: File Form 843 the way the IRS now asks

Refunds in this situation are claimed on Form 843, Claim for Refund and Request for Abatement, one form per tax period. Since July 1, 2026 the IRS has special instructions for Kwong claims:

  1. Complete a Form 843 for each tax year or period where COVID-window penalties or interest were paid. List the payment dates and amounts from your transcript.
  2. Write “Kwong vs. United States” across the top, the wording the IRS uses to route these claims. In the explanation, cite the pending Kwong litigation over IRC § 7508A(d) as the basis, and say the claim is filed to protect your rights while the appeal is decided.
  3. Individuals: you can file online. With an IRS Online Account, you can submit a Kwong claim electronically for fully paid penalties and interest. Save the confirmation.
  4. Businesses (and anyone filing on paper): mail it to Ogden. Internal Revenue Service, 1973 N Rulon White Blvd., Ogden, UT 84201. Send it certified mail, return receipt requested. Under IRC § 7502 the postmark date is your filing date. Keep copies of everything.
  5. Then wait, possibly years. The Federal Circuit's decision, and possibly the Supreme Court's, will decide whether these claims are paid. Your claim keeps your limitations period from expiring while everyone waits. If you receive a denial instead, see what to do.

Romeo Razi · Former IRS Tax Examiner · ★ 5.0 on Google reviews →

"Be precise about payment dates. That's the first thing an IRS examiner looks at on a claim like this, because it decides whether the claim is timely at all. A vague claim with no dates is an easy one to reject."

Changed since July: unpaid penalties

Earlier guidance (including ours) suggested filing protective requests to abate COVID-window penalties you haven't paid yet. The IRS isn't holding those. Practitioners report it answers them with Letter 916C (“no consideration”), and the IRS's online tool only accepts fully paid amounts. In practice the claim follows the payment: each payment you make on those penalties and interest opens its own 2-year window to file. If Kwong is upheld, an unpaid balance can be adjusted then.

Missed July 10, 2026? You may still get an IRS COVID penalty refund

July 10, 2026 was the general "most taxpayers" date — it was never a single universal statutory deadline. Your personal deadline under IRC § 6511 is the later of:

  • 3 years from the date you filed the return, or
  • 2 years from the date you paid the tax (and each payment starts its own 2-year clock).

Example: you owe $55,000 for 2021, including COVID-window penalties and interest, and pay it off on September 9, 2026. You have until September 9, 2028 to file a Kwong claim for the penalties and interest in that payment.

That means claims can still be timely after July 10, 2026 for people who paid COVID-era penalties within the last two years — including through installment agreement payments, levies, and refund offsets — for people who filed the underlying returns late, and for people with open audits, appeals, or litigation. Kwong vs. the new Automatic Penalty Relief (AEP) — don't confuse them

Two days before the Kwong deadline, on July 8, 2026, the IRS announced its new Automatic Exemption from Penalty (AEP) program. They are completely different animals, and you can potentially benefit from both:

Kwong v. US COVID penalty refund: frequently asked questions

What is the Kwong decision, in one sentence?
A federal court held that the tax code automatically postponed filing and payment deadlines for the entire COVID disaster period (Jan 20, 2020 – Jul 10, 2023), which — if upheld — means late-filing and late-payment penalties and related interest assessed for that window shouldn't have been charged.
Is this real, or another "IRS fresh start" gimmick?
It's real: the source is the National Taxpayer Advocate (an independent office inside the IRS), who publicly urged taxpayers to file claims before July 10, 2026, and more than 275,000 people have. But it is also genuinely uncertain: the government is appealing the ruling at the Federal Circuit. Anyone who guarantees you a refund is misleading you. What's certain is the procedure: a timely claim preserves your rights; no claim means no refund even if taxpayers win.
How much money could this be worth?
It depends entirely on your account. The failure-to-file penalty alone runs 5% per month up to 25% of the unpaid tax; failure-to-pay adds 0.5% per month; interest compounds on top. Someone who filed a 2019 or 2020 return a year late with a $20,000 balance could easily have $5,000–$7,000+ in COVID-window penalties and interest at stake. Your account transcripts give the exact figures.
What's the status of the Kwong appeal?
Pending at the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, Kwong v. United States, No. 26-1843. The government appealed on May 15, 2026 and filed its opening brief in September 2026, arguing the automatic postponement lasted only 60 days. Mr. Kwong's response and oral argument come next. A decision is unlikely before 2027, and the losing side can ask the Supreme Court to review it.
The IRS denied my Kwong claim. Is it over?
Not necessarily. The National Taxpayer Advocate asked the IRS in October 2026 to stop denials and hold claims until the courts rule. But a formal notice of disallowance generally starts a two-year deadline to file a refund suit, and talking with the IRS doesn't pause it. Note the date and follow these steps.
Can I still get an IRS COVID penalty refund after July 10, 2026?
Possibly. July 10, 2026 was not the deadline for everyone. It was the conservative "most taxpayers" date. The real limitations rule (IRC § 6511) is 3 years from filing or 2 years from payment, whichever is later — so recent payments, late-filed returns, and open exams/appeals can keep the window open. See our missed-deadline guide.
Can I e-file Form 843 for a Kwong claim?
Since July 1, 2026, yes, if you're an individual with an IRS Online Account and the penalties and interest are fully paid. Businesses still file on paper: write “Kwong vs. United States” across the top and mail it certified to Internal Revenue Service, 1973 N Rulon White Blvd., Ogden, UT 84201. The postmark is your proof of timely filing under IRC § 7502.
I'm on an installment agreement that includes COVID-era penalties. Does this affect me?
Yes, for what you've paid. Payments made in the last two years that were applied to COVID-window penalties and interest can support a still-timely refund claim, and every future payment opens its own 2-year window. The unpaid part is different: the IRS isn't holding requests to abate unpaid amounts. If Kwong holds, your remaining balance could shrink then. This intersects with how your agreement is structured, so it's worth a professional look.
Does this apply to state tax penalties?
No. Kwong interprets a federal statute (IRC § 7508A) and applies to federal penalties and interest only. States have their own rules and their own COVID relief programs, which are separate.
What if I already received First Time Abate or reasonable-cause relief for one of these years?
Then part of your penalty exposure may already be resolved — but interest, other periods, and other penalty types may remain. It also raises a strategic question: if Kwong ultimately wipes a penalty by operation of law, relief you "spent" on that year might have been usable elsewhere. This is exactly the kind of multi-year cleanup where a professional review pays for itself.
