Form 5500 Late?
Here's What It Costs

Form 5500-EZ late filing penalty: the $250-a-day IRS fine, the DOL’s, and the $500 fix

The short answer: A late Form 5500 can draw penalties from two different agencies at once. The IRS penalty is $250 per day, up to $150,000 per return, plus interest (IRC §6652(e)). The Department of Labor (DOL) penalty runs up to $2,739 per day, with no maximum (ERISA §502(c)(2)). Which fix applies depends on your plan: standard plans with employees use the DOL's DFVCP program to cap the DOL fee and coordinate IRS relief; one-participant plans — solo 401(k)s and similar — use the IRS's Rev. Proc. 2015-32 program instead, a flat $500 per return, capped at $1,500 per plan. Both programs close once the IRS or DOL formally assesses the penalty — after that, your only path is reasonable cause abatement. The rest of this guide covers both routes; if you're specifically behind on a solo 401(k)'s Form 5500-EZ, jump to the $500 fix, step by step.

Nothing has been assessed yet, so the $500 relief program is fully open — and filing now costs the same $500 whether you're one week late or one year late. If the IRS sent a CP 216H, your extension was denied because the 5558 arrived after July 31; your penalty clock started August 1, not October 16.

The two penalties: IRS vs. DOL

Every Form 5500-series filing gets reported to both the IRS and the Department of Labor, and a late or missing return can draw a penalty from each of them, independently, for the same return. They don't share a cap, and paying one doesn't satisfy the other.

Who's liableThe plan administrator, personally — usually the business owner or a designated fiduciary

The good news: satisfying the DOL's relief program for a given return also helps coordinate abatement of the related IRS penalty, so you're generally not paying both in full if you act voluntarily. Which program you use depends entirely on your plan type.

Why this figure keeps changing

The DOL penalty is normally adjusted for inflation every year under the Federal Civil Penalties Inflation Adjustment Act. 2026 is the exception: the adjustment is tied to October CPI data, BLS never published October 2025 because of the shutdown, and OMB told agencies to keep 2025 amounts — so $2,739/day still applies through 2026. Expect the DOL to revisit the figure in 2027. The IRS's $250/day and $150,000 cap, by contrast, are fixed statutory amounts set by the SECURE Act and don't move annually.

The filing requirement most solo 401(k) owners never hear about

Form 5500-EZ is the annual return for one-participant retirement plans — solo 401(k)s covering only a business owner (and spouse) or partners (and their spouses). Two triggers create the duty to file:

  • The $250,000 threshold: total assets across your one-participant plans exceed $250,000 on the last day of the plan year.
  • The final-year rule: the year a plan terminates and distributes all assets, a final return is required regardless of balance — even a $61,000 plan owes a final 5500-EZ.

The due date is the last day of the seventh month after the plan year ends — July 31 for calendar-year plans. Form 5558, filed by July 31, buys an automatic extension to October 15.

Why so many people miss it: contributions take years to cross $250,000, but a single rollover — say, moving an old IRA into the solo 401(k) as step one of a backdoor Roth — can blow past the threshold overnight. And your custodian does not file this for you. When the IRS sampled one-participant plan filers, most sponsors contacted had missed at least one 5500-EZ.

The penalty math is genuinely brutal

  • $250 per day, up to $150,000 per late return, plus interest — IRC § 6652(e), increased tenfold by Section 403 of the SECURE Act (it was $25/day, $15,000 max, for returns due before 2020).
  • You hit the $150,000 cap after just 600 days. Three missed years can theoretically stack to $450,000.
  • The plan administrator — you personally — is liable.

NoticeWhat it means

The $500 fix: Rev. Proc. 2015-32, step by step

The IRS maintains a permanent penalty relief program for one-participant and foreign plans. Instead of $250/day, you pay a flat $500 per delinquent return, capped at $1,500 per plan no matter how many years you missed — so filing multiple late years together is the money move.

  1. Confirm you actually owed the filing. Assets over $250,000 at year-end, or a final-year return? If your plan held $180,000 and isn't terminating, you had no duty — stop here.
  2. Prepare a complete paper Form 5500-EZ for each delinquent year, using that year's form.
  3. Check Box D in Part I ("IRS Late Filer Penalty Relief Program"). For pre-2020 forms without Box D, write "Delinquent Return Filed under Rev. Proc. 2015-32, Eligible for Penalty Relief" in red at the top — miss this and the submission can be ineligible.
  4. Attach Form 14704 (the transmittal schedule) to the front of the oldest return in the submission.
  5. Include the fee: $500 per return, max $1,500 per plan, check payable to "United States Treasury."
  6. Mail on paper to the IRS in Ogden, UT (1973 Rulon White Blvd., Ogden, UT 84201). Electronically filed EFAST2 returns are not eligible for this relief. Send certified mail and keep everything.

Two disqualifiers: (1) you already received a CP 283 for that year, or (2) your plan covers any employee beyond owners and spouses — that makes it an ERISA Title I plan, which uses the Department of Labor's DFVCP instead. Note: 2%+ S corporation shareholders are treated like partners for the one-participant test.

Got a CP 403, CP 406 or CP 214? What each one means for the $500 fix

The IRS updated its relief-program FAQ on August 26, 2026, and its notice guide on July 23, 2026. Together they settle the question we get most: "I already got a letter — is it too late?" Usually, no.

Three details from the IRS FAQ worth knowing

  • A denied reasonable-cause request locks you out. If you ask the IRS to waive the penalty for reasonable cause and it says no, you're no longer eligible for the $500 program for that return. If the program is still open to you, use it first.
  • E-filed the late return by mistake? Late returns sent through EFAST2 aren't eligible — but if no CP 283 has been issued for that return yet, the IRS says you may still submit a paper relief request.
  • Use the address in the current instructions. The FAQ tells filers to mail to the address in the most recent Form 5500-EZ instructions rather than a fixed address. The Ogden address in our steps above is the one in use as of this update; check the instructions before you mail.

Already got a penalty notice? Reasonable cause is the remaining path

Once either agency formally assesses a penalty — a CP 283 from the IRS, or a determination letter from the DOL — the voluntary programs above are off the table for that return. But the penalty is still fightable with both agencies. The standard is reasonable cause: you exercised ordinary business care and prudence and still couldn't comply. Documented serious illness, death in the family, disaster, or records you genuinely couldn't obtain are the classic fact patterns. Respond within the notice deadline with a signed statement and evidence; if denied, you can take it to the IRS Independent Office of Appeals.

Romeo’s take — Romeo Razi, Former IRS Tax Examiner · ★ 5.0 on Google reviews

"I've watched five-figure 5500-EZ penalties get assessed against people whose only mistake was a rollover they didn't know crossed a threshold. Here's the pattern that matters: the IRS's own program forgives almost everything for $500 a year — but only until the CP 283 lands. The single most expensive thing you can do is let mail from Ogden sit unopened. If you're behind, file under the relief program this week, not after the IRS writes first. And when a penalty is abated, remember the interest that accrued on it comes off too."

Paper submissions and slow IRS mail: protect yourself

The relief program is paper-only, and right now IRS mail moves slowly. In Romeo's recent interview with enrolled agent Yoav Betsion, Yoav described documents his office sent to an IRS service center that sat unopened for seven months — until an IRS employee finally admitted no one had touched the file. Romeo described a client's refund that took six to nine months and multiple calls to get reissued. For a 5500-EZ relief submission, that means:

  • Send it certified mail, return receipt requested, and keep a full copy of everything — every return, Form 14704 and the check.
  • Note the date your check clears. It's practical proof the IRS received your submission.
  • If a CP 403 or CP 406 arrives after you've mailed, respond within 30 days with a copy of your submission and your mailing proof — don't assume the two will get matched up on their own.
  • If months pass with no response and a notice threatens penalties, the Taxpayer Advocate Service can help when a case is stuck.

Frequently asked questions

I got a CP 403 for a missing Form 5500-EZ. Can I still use the $500 relief program?
Yes. The IRS's relief-program FAQ says a delinquency notice (CP 403 or CP 406) does not disqualify you. Only a CP 283 — the notice that the penalty has been charged — closes the program for that return. Respond by filing the late return on paper under Rev. Proc. 2015-32 with Form 14704 and the $500 fee.
I missed the October 15 extended deadline for my 5500-EZ. What now?
If you filed Form 5558 for a calendar-year 2025 plan, the extension ran out October 15, 2026, and the $250-per-day IRS penalty started the next day. As long as no CP 283 has been issued, you can file under the IRS penalty relief program for a flat $500 per return, capped at $1,500 per plan. If you received a CP 216H, your extension was denied and the penalty clock runs from July 31 instead.
Did the DOL Form 5500 penalty increase in 2026?
No. The Department of Labor canceled its 2026 inflation adjustment for civil penalties in a May 27, 2026 Federal Register notice, because the October 2025 CPI data the law requires was never published after the government shutdown. The maximum DOL penalty for a late Form 5500 remains $2,739 per day for 2026, and the DOL plans a full review of its civil penalties in 2027.
Should I request reasonable cause or use the $500 program first?
Use the $500 program first if it's still open to you (no CP 283 issued). Under the IRS's FAQ, if you request reasonable-cause relief and it is denied, you are no longer eligible for the penalty relief program for that return. Reasonable cause is the fallback once a CP 283 has been issued.
What is the penalty for late filing of Form 5500?
A late Form 5500 triggers two separate penalties. The IRS penalty is $250 per day, up to $150,000 per return, under IRC Section 6652(e). The DOL penalty is up to $2,739 per day with no maximum. Both regulators can assess independently for the same late filing, and the plan administrator is personally liable. Two relief programs exist to fix this before either penalty is assessed: DFVCP for standard, multi-participant plans, and Rev. Proc. 2015-32 (a flat $500 per return) for one-participant plans like solo 401(k)s.
What is the DOL penalty for a late Form 5500, and how is it different from the IRS penalty?
The DOL penalty, assessed under ERISA Section 502(c)(2), can run up to $2,739 per day late with no statutory maximum — uncapped, unlike the IRS's $150,000 ceiling. The two penalties are separate and can both apply to the same late return, from two different agencies. Filing under DFVCP before either agency assesses replaces the DOL penalty with a capped fee, and satisfying DFVCP also helps coordinate relief from the related IRS penalty.
What is DFVCP and how much does it cost?
The Delinquent Filer Voluntary Compliance Program (DFVCP) is the DOL's voluntary correction program for standard, multi-participant ERISA plans that missed a Form 5500 or 5500-SF deadline — the equivalent of the IRS's Rev. Proc. 2015-32 program for solo 401(k)s. The fee is $10 per day late, capped at $750 per late return and $1,500 per plan for a small plan (under 100 participants), or $2,000 per return and $4,000 per plan for a large plan, well below the DOL's uncapped daily penalty. Completing DFVCP also helps coordinate abatement of the related IRS penalty. It isn't available for one-participant plans, which use Rev. Proc. 2015-32 instead. Confirm current fee caps on the DOL's DFVCP page before filing, since program fees are periodically adjusted.
Who is exempt from filing Form 5500?
The main exemptions: most governmental plans and church plans (unless the church elected ERISA coverage) don't file at all. Fully insured or unfunded welfare benefit plans with fewer than 100 participants — think a small group health plan with no separate trust — are also generally exempt. And one-participant plans, like a solo 401(k), don't owe a filing until plan assets exceed $250,000 at year-end or the plan terminates. If none of those apply and your plan has employee participants, you almost certainly owe a Form 5500 — confirm with a plan administrator or ERISA counsel if you're unsure, since exemption rules have edge cases.
What counts as reasonable cause for late filing Form 5500?
Reasonable cause means you exercised ordinary business care and prudence but were still prevented from filing on time by circumstances beyond your control — for example, a natural disaster or fire that destroyed records, a serious illness or death in the immediate family, or records you genuinely could not obtain despite diligent effort. Simply not knowing about the filing requirement generally does not qualify. It's evaluated case by case after a penalty notice arrives — a fallback once the voluntary programs are no longer available for that year, not a substitute for filing on time.
Who has to file Form 5500-EZ?
One-participant retirement plans — a solo 401(k) or similar plan covering only a business owner (and spouse), or partners (and their spouses), with no common-law employees. You must file for any year the plan's total assets exceed $250,000 on the last day of the plan year, and you must always file a final return in the year the plan terminates and distributes all assets, regardless of the balance.
When is Form 5500-EZ due?
The last day of the seventh month after the plan year ends — July 31 for calendar-year plans. You can extend to October 15 by filing Form 5558 by July 31. Miss July 31 without an extension and you're delinquent on day one, with the $250-per-day clock running.
What is the penalty for filing Form 5500-EZ late?
$250 per day, up to $150,000 per late return, plus interest, under IRC Section 6652(e) as amended by the SECURE Act (a tenfold increase from the old $25/day, $15,000 cap). You hit the $150,000 maximum after just 600 days, and the plan administrator — you personally — is liable. Three missed years can theoretically stack to $450,000.
What is the $500 fix for a late 5500-EZ?
The IRS Penalty Relief Program under Revenue Procedure 2015-32: file the delinquent returns on paper with Form 14704 attached and pay a flat $500 per late return, capped at $1,500 per plan no matter how many years you missed. The IRS waives the daily penalty entirely. It's a permanent program, not a one-time amnesty.
How do I file under Rev. Proc. 2015-32 step by step?
(1) Prepare a complete paper Form 5500-EZ for each delinquent year. (2) Check Box D in Part I ('IRS Late Filer Penalty Relief Program'); for pre-2020 form years without Box D, write 'Delinquent Return Filed under Rev. Proc. 2015-32, Eligible for Penalty Relief' in red at the top. (3) Attach Form 14704 to the front of the submission. (4) Include a check for $500 per return (max $1,500 per plan) payable to United States Treasury. (5) Mail to the IRS in Ogden, UT — electronic EFAST2 filings are NOT eligible. Keep certified-mail proof.
What if I already received a CP 283 penalty notice?
Then the $500 program is closed for that year — Rev. Proc. 2015-32 is only available before the IRS assesses the penalty. Your remaining path is reasonable cause abatement: respond to the CP 283 within the deadline with a documented explanation (serious illness, disaster, records you couldn't obtain despite ordinary business care). If denied, you can appeal to the IRS Independent Office of Appeals. This is exactly the kind of case a former IRS auditor handles.
What notices does the IRS send for a missing 5500-EZ?
Roughly 15 months after the due date you may get a CP 403 delinquency notice — by which point the daily penalty math already sits around $112,500. If you don't respond in 30 days, a CP 406 final notice follows about 15 weeks later. If the IRS then assesses, you receive CP 283 (Penalty Charged on Your Form 5500 Return) — and the cheap $500 door closes for that year. Never let mail from Ogden sit unopened.
I never filed because I didn't know my solo 401(k) crossed $250,000 — am I alone?
Not remotely. The IRS sampled one-participant plan filers and found most sponsors contacted had missed at least one Form 5500-EZ. The classic trigger is a rollover: moving an old IRA or 401(k) into your solo 401(k) — a common step in backdoor Roth planning — can push the balance over $250,000 overnight. And no, your custodian (Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard) does not file this for you; the responsibility is yours as plan administrator.
Does the relief program cover plans with employees?
No. The moment a plan covers any employee beyond the owners and their spouses, it's an ERISA Title I plan and uses the Department of Labor's Delinquent Filer Voluntary Compliance Program (DFVCP) instead. Note that S corporation shareholders owning 2%+ are treated like partners for the one-participant test.
Do I even owe a filing? How do I check before paying $500?
Verify two things first: (1) did combined assets across all your one-participant plans exceed $250,000 on the last day of the plan year, and (2) is this the plan's final year (final-year returns are required at ANY balance)? If your solo 401(k) held $180,000 at year-end and it isn't the final year, you had no filing duty — don't pay a relief fee you don't owe.
