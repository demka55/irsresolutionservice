Which "2027" do you actually mean?

This is the single most common point of confusion in this entire topic, and almost no page addresses it clearly. There are two completely different things people mean when they search "2027 tax brackets":

If you mean...
"What brackets apply to the return I'm filing in 2027?"

You want the tax-year-2026 brackets. These are already official — released as Revenue Procedure 2025-32 on October 9, 2025. Full tables are directly below.
If you mean...
"What brackets apply to income I'll earn during 2027?"

These are the true tax-year-2027 brackets — filed in 2028. Now official — released by the IRS on November 5, 2026. Tables are further down this page.

2027 standard deduction, the 65+ deduction and the $6,000 senior deduction

The standard deduction is the first thing subtracted from your income, before any bracket applies. The IRS set the 2027 amounts at the same time as the brackets.

Extra deductions if you are 65 or older or blind

  • Additional standard deduction: $1,700 per qualifying condition for married filers, $2,100 if you are unmarried and not a surviving spouse. Each spouse who qualifies gets their own.
  • The $6,000 senior deduction (added by the 2025 tax law): $6,000 per eligible person age 65 or older, for tax years 2025 through 2028. It shrinks by 6 cents for each dollar of modified adjusted gross income above $75,000 (single) or $150,000 (joint), and is gone for a single filer at $175,000. It is not indexed, you can take it whether or not you itemize, and it does not depend on claiming Social Security.
  • Dependents: a person who can be claimed by someone else is limited to the greater of $1,400 or earned income plus $500.

  • Head of household standard deduction: the IRS figure is $24,925. Some early videos and posts say $24,950, and some pre-release projections said $24,900.

How the 2027 brackets hit retirees with pensions: three worked examples

What you pay depends less on which bracket you are in and more on how your income fills the brackets. Picture each bracket as a bucket: income fills the 10% bucket first, then the 12% bucket, then 22%, and only the dollars in each bucket are taxed at its rate. A pension fills the cheap buckets before you touch a dollar of savings.

Why inflation indexing helps less than it looks

The brackets rose about 3.2% because the IRS indexes to chained CPI, while Social Security's cost-of-living adjustment is 3.6%. Some numbers aren't indexed at all: the $6,000 senior deduction, the Social Security taxation thresholds ($25,000/$34,000 single, $32,000/$44,000 joint) and the 3.8% net investment income tax thresholds. That is why the over-65 couple in the first example saves only about $318 versus 2026 while the under-65 couple saves $561.

What actually changes for tax year 2027 — beyond the brackets

Almost every "2027 tax changes" page just re-runs the inflation math on brackets and the standard deduction. That's real, but it's not the whole story. A handful of OBBBA provisions have their own separate 2027 start dates, written directly into the statute — some take their first real step in 2027, others are brand new and don't exist at all until 2027, and others are simply still in effect and worth confirming haven't quietly expired. Here's the detailed breakdown of all three.

New in 2027: provisions taking their first real step

Opportunity Zones become a permanent, restructured program — "OZ 2.0" — starting January 1, 2027. This is the single biggest substantive change tied specifically to the 2027 calendar, and almost no consumer tax-bracket page mentions it. Under the original 2017 program, deferred capital gains had to be recognized by December 31, 2026 at the latest, regardless of when the investment was made — existing OZ investors face that mandatory recognition event at the end of this year. For any new investment made on or after January 1, 2027, OBBBA replaces that fixed deadline with a permanent, rolling structure:

  • Rolling 5-year deferral — deferred gain is recognized at the earlier of selling the investment or its 5th anniversary, not a fixed calendar date. Someone investing in 2027 and someone investing in 2032 each get their own full 5-year clock.
  • 10% basis step-up at the 5-year mark for standard Qualified Opportunity Funds — simplified from the old two-tier structure (10% at 5 years, an additional 5% at 7 years).
  • 30% basis step-up at 5 years for a new category, Qualified Rural Opportunity Funds (QROFs) — plus a lower "substantial improvement" threshold (50% instead of 100%) for rural properties.
  • Continued tax-free appreciation on gains from a qualifying investment held at least 10 years, now capped so the stepped-up basis freezes at fair market value on the 30th anniversary.
  • Tighter, redesignated zones — new census-tract eligibility criteria apply starting 2027, and zone designations now roll over every 10 years instead of being fixed indefinitely.

A brand-new federal tax credit — the Federal Scholarship Tax Credit (FSTC) — starts January 1, 2027, and didn't exist before. This one is confirmed directly on the IRS's own website, not just secondary analysis: individual taxpayers can claim a nonrefundable federal credit worth 100% of qualifying cash contributions, up to $1,700 per taxpayer per year, for donations to IRS-approved Scholarship Granting Organizations (SGOs) — nonprofits that fund K-12 private-school scholarships. A few mechanics worth knowing:

  • State opt-in required. Your state has to formally elect to participate and certify a list of qualifying SGOs before you can claim it — the IRS reports more than two dozen states have already made the advance election for 2027.
  • Dollar-for-dollar, not a deduction. Because it's a credit against 100% of the contribution (not a percentage-based deduction), it can effectively make the donation cost-free for a taxpayer who owes enough tax to absorb the full $1,700.
  • No double-dipping. If you also receive a state tax credit for the same contribution, your federal credit is reduced accordingly, and you can't also claim the contribution as a charitable deduction.
  • You'll first claim it when filing your 2027 return — i.e., in early 2028 — but the contributions themselves can start being made as soon as your state's SGO list is certified, expected around January 1, 2027.

The AMT exemption phase-out gets its first inflation adjustment in 2027 — and it's still catching up from a 2026 rate change. For 2026, the phase-out threshold is fixed at $500,000 (single) / $1,000,000 (MFJ), and OBBBA doubled the phase-out rate from 25% to 50% of income above that threshold — meaning the AMT exemption now disappears twice as fast once you cross it (fully eliminated at $680,200 single / $1,280,400 MFJ for 2026). Starting with 2027, that $500,000/$1,000,000 threshold begins adjusting for inflation each year going forward, at the faster 50% phase-out rate.

Three more provisions get their first inflation adjustment in 2027, after being fixed dollar amounts since 2026:

  • Estate, gift, and GST tax exemption — set at a flat $15 million (single) / $30 million (married) starting 2026, with annual inflation indexing beginning in 2027.
  • QSBS (qualified small business stock) gain exclusion — OBBBA raised the exclusion cap from $10 million to $15 million (or 10x basis, if greater), with inflation adjustments starting in 2027, and separately raised the company-size limit for eligibility from $50 million to $75 million in gross assets, also inflation-indexed from 2027. It also added a new tiered exclusion for stock that doesn't meet the full 5-year hold: 50% exclusion at 3 years, 75% at 4 years, 100% at 5+ years — a meaningful change from the old all-or-nothing 5-year cliff.
  • QBI minimum deduction floor — the new $400 minimum deduction for taxpayers with at least $1,000 of qualifying business income, inflation-adjusted starting in 2027.

The SALT deduction cap continues its scheduled 1% annual increase. The cap is $40,000 for 2025, confirmed at $40,400 for 2026, and rises by a further 1% for tax years 2027 through 2029 before reverting to $10,000 in 2030. Applying that exact statutory 1% to the confirmed 2026 figure puts the 2027 cap at $40,804 — a precise calculation from the formula Congress wrote, though only the IRS's own guidance makes it official — with the MAGI phase-out threshold (2026: $505,000) rising to roughly $510,000.

Smaller but real: ACA marketplace premium tax credit eligibility narrows further for certain lawfully present immigrants starting in 2027, part of a phased set of eligibility restrictions that began in 2026 — worth a specific look if that applies to your household.

The bracket table the IRS uses if you don't file

Every table on this page assumes you file a return. If you don't, the IRS can file one for you — a Substitute for Return (SFR) built from the W-2s and 1099s it already has. An SFR typically uses single or married-filing-separately status and leaves out the business expenses, dependents and credits you'd claim yourself. For a married non-filer, that usually means the separate-filer table above — where the 37% rate starts at $396,825 instead of $793,650 for a joint return.

You keep the right to replace an SFR with your own original return. In a recent interview with Romeo, Yoav Betsion, EA — an enrolled agent who has handled IRS collection cases for 20 years — described a client who hadn't filed for 2016: the IRS's substitute return showed a balance of more than $400,000, and preparing the actual return cut about $30,000 from that one year alone.

Romeo Razi — Former IRS Auditor

"When I worked at the IRS, an SFR was built to establish a number, not to give you every break you're entitled to — no filing status choice, no business expenses unless you prove them. People look at bracket tables and worry about a $50 threshold shift, while the most expensive bracket decision they'll ever face is letting the IRS pick the table for them. If you have unfiled years, filing your own return is almost always the cheapest option."

The basics

Are the 2027 tax brackets official?

Short answer: Yes. The IRS released the tax year 2027 inflation adjustments on November 5, 2026, in news release IR-2026-133 and Revenue Procedure 2026-35. They apply to income earned in 2027, on the return you file in early 2028.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysThese are final numbers, not estimates. I read the Revenue Procedure itself rather than relying on news summaries, because the summaries leave out head of household, married filing separately and capital gains. Everything on this page now comes from the primary source.
When did the IRS release the 2027 tax brackets, and was it late?

Short answer: November 5, 2026. That's about four weeks after last year's October 9 release, but still inside the October 9 – November 15 window the IRS has hit every year for more than a decade.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysPeople panic when the release slips a few weeks. Don't. The bracket math is a formula, not a negotiation, and the IRS has never missed mid-November in the cycles I've checked. Late October versus early November changes nothing about your tax bill.
Did the 2027 tax rates change?

Short answer: No. The seven rates are still 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35% and 37%. What moved is the income level where each rate starts, up about 3.2% on average.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysRates are set by Congress, and the 2025 law made this structure permanent. The IRS only adjusts the thresholds for inflation. So when a headline says 'new tax brackets,' read it as 'same rates, wider buckets.'
Which tax year do the 2027 brackets apply to?

Short answer: Income you earn in calendar year 2027, reported on the return you file in early 2028. If you're filing a return this coming spring, you need the 2026 figures instead.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysThis is the single most common mix-up I see. 'Tax brackets 2027' searches are often from people who really want the 2026 table for the return they file in 2027. If that's you, use our confirmed 2026 page, then come back here for planning.
How much did the brackets go up for 2027?

Short answer: About 3.2% on average. Most thresholds rose between 3.2% and 3.3%, and the standard deductions rose about 3.1%, because the IRS rounds each figure separately.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysDon't expect every number to move by exactly the same percentage. Rounding rules push individual thresholds a little above or below the average. That's normal and it's how the projections ended up so close to the final numbers.

2027 tax brackets and standard deduction

What are the 2027 tax brackets for single filers?

Short answer: 10% up to $12,800; 12% to $52,025; 22% to $109,125; 24% to $208,325; 32% to $264,550; 35% to $661,375; 37% above $661,375.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysRemember these apply to taxable income, meaning what's left after your standard or itemized deductions. A single filer earning $85,000 isn't in the 22% bracket on all of it. Only the slice above $52,025 of taxable income is.
What are the 2027 tax brackets for married couples filing jointly?

Short answer: 10% up to $25,600; 12% to $104,050; 22% to $218,250; 24% to $416,650; 32% to $529,100; 35% to $793,650; 37% above $793,650.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysFor the first five brackets, the joint numbers are exactly double the single ones. The 35% and 37% brackets don't follow that pattern, which is what the marriage penalty and bonus conversation is really about for high earners.
What are the 2027 tax brackets for head of household?

Short answer: 10% up to $18,250; 12% to $69,650; 22% to $109,100; 24% to $208,300; 32% to $264,550; 35% to $661,350; 37% above $661,350.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysHead of household sits between single and joint at the bottom and matches single at the top. If you qualify, it's usually worth the paperwork, because the lower brackets are wider and the standard deduction is $8,325 bigger than single. The IRS does check qualifying-person rules, so be sure you meet them.
What are the 2027 tax brackets for married filing separately?

Short answer: The same as single through the 32% bracket (10% to $12,800, 12% to $52,025, 22% to $109,125, 24% to $208,325, 32% to $264,550). The 35% bracket ends at $396,825, and 37% applies above that, which is exactly half the joint threshold.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysFiling separately rarely saves money, and it can cost you credits and deductions. It's also the status the IRS tends to use when it builds a substitute return for a married person who didn't file, which is a nasty surprise.
What is the standard deduction for 2027?

Short answer: $16,600 for single filers and married filing separately, $33,200 for married filing jointly, and $24,925 for head of household. That's up $500, $1,000 and $775 from 2026.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysThe standard deduction is income you never pay federal tax on. Most people take it. Itemize only if your deductible mortgage interest, state and local taxes, charity and medical costs beat it. If you're close, run both.
Is the 2027 head of household standard deduction $24,925 or $24,950?

Short answer: $24,925, according to Revenue Procedure 2026-35. Some early videos and posts say $24,950, and some pre-release projections said $24,900. Neither is the IRS figure.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysWe got this one wrong ourselves: before the release we assumed $24,925 had to be a typo because we applied a $50 rounding rule. It doesn't apply to that figure, and the IRS published $24,925. When a number looks wrong, go to the Revenue Procedure.
What's the extra standard deduction if I'm 65 or older or blind?

Short answer: $1,700 per qualifying condition if you're married, or $2,100 if you're unmarried and not a surviving spouse. Each spouse who qualifies gets their own.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysThis one stacks. A single filer who's both 65 and blind can claim it twice. It's separate from the new $6,000 senior deduction below, and you get both if you qualify.
Why did the brackets rise only about 3.2% when inflation looked higher?

Short answer: The IRS indexes brackets to chained CPI (C-CPI-U), which usually runs a little below the headline CPI. For comparison, Social Security's 2027 cost-of-living adjustment is 3.6%.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysChained CPI assumes you switch to cheaper options as prices rise, so it grows slower than the number in the news. Over time that makes brackets grow slightly slower than your real cost of living, which is a quiet form of bracket creep. It's small in any single year and adds up over decades.
Does the 2027 adjustment actually cut my taxes?

Short answer: Slightly, if your income stays flat. A single filer with $85,000 of gross income pays about $281 less under 2027 brackets than 2026 brackets. A married couple with $150,000 under 65 pays $561 less.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysThink of it as the government agreeing not to take more of your raise just because of inflation, not as a gift. If your income rises about 3%, your tax bill rises about 3% too, and your effective rate stays about the same.
How does the $6,000 senior deduction work in 2027?

Short answer: It's a $6,000 deduction per eligible person age 65 or older, available through tax year 2028. It shrinks by 6 cents per dollar of modified adjusted gross income above $75,000 (single) or $150,000 (joint), and it's fully gone for a single filer at $175,000.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysIt doesn't depend on whether you claim Social Security, and you can take it whether you itemize or not. It isn't indexed for inflation, so $6,000 stays $6,000. For joint filers with both spouses eligible, read the form instructions closely on how the phase-out is applied.

How brackets actually work

If I move into a higher bracket, is all my income taxed at that rate?

Short answer: No. Only the dollars inside each bracket are taxed at that bracket's rate. A raise can never reduce your take-home pay because of brackets.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysI hear 'I don't want a raise, it'll push me into a higher bracket' more than almost any other tax myth. Picture buckets. Your income fills the 10% bucket first, then the 12% bucket, and so on. Only the dollars that land in a bucket are taxed at that bucket's rate.
Can you show a real 2027 example of how the brackets work?

Short answer: A married couple under 65 with $150,000 of income subtracts the $33,200 standard deduction and has $116,800 of taxable income. They owe $2,560 on the first $25,600 (10%), $9,414 on the next $78,450 (12%), and $2,805 on the last $12,750 (22%), for $14,779 total.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysThat couple's marginal rate is 22%, but their effective rate, tax divided by income, is just under 10%. Those are two different numbers and people mix them up all the time. Planning decisions use the marginal rate. Budgeting uses the effective rate.
What's the difference between marginal and effective tax rate?

Short answer: The marginal rate is the rate on your next dollar of taxable income. The effective rate is your total tax divided by your total income. In 2027 the example couple above has a 22% marginal rate and a 9.9% effective rate.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysWhen I'm deciding whether a Roth conversion, a bonus or a side gig makes sense, I look at the marginal rate. When a client asks 'what do I really pay,' I show them the effective rate. Both matter, for different questions.
Do the 2027 brackets apply to capital gains?

Short answer: Not directly. Long-term capital gains use their own 0%, 15% and 20% brackets (below). Short-term gains, from assets held a year or less, are taxed as ordinary income at the regular brackets.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysCapital gains get stacked on top of your ordinary income. They never push your ordinary income into a higher bracket, but they can push the gain itself from 0% to 15%. Think of it as your ordinary income using up the 0% room first.

Retirees, pensions and Social Security

How does the 2027 tax picture look for a married couple age 65 or older?

Short answer: With the $33,200 standard deduction, $1,700 extra per spouse and the $6,000 senior deduction each, such a couple can shelter $48,600 of income. On $150,000 of income that leaves $101,400 taxable and a $11,656 tax bill, about $3,100 less than a couple under 65.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysAge matters more in the tax code than most people realize. The same income can produce a very different bill at 64 and at 65. If you're a couple years from retirement, run the numbers for the year you turn 65, not just this year.
How is Social Security taxed in 2027?

Short answer: Up to 85% of your benefits can be taxable, depending on your provisional income: other income plus half your Social Security. The thresholds, $25,000 and $34,000 for single filers and $32,000 and $44,000 for joint filers, were set decades ago and are not indexed for inflation.

Romeo Razi, CPA says85% taxable doesn't mean an 85% tax. It means up to 85% of your benefit is added to taxable income, then taxed at your normal brackets. Because those thresholds never move, more retirees get caught every year as incomes rise. It's one of the least-understood parts of retirement taxes.
Can you show how a single retiree with a pension would be taxed in 2027?

Short answer: Take Greg, 66 and single, with a $40,000 pension, $20,000 of Social Security and $20,000 from pre-tax savings. About $17,000 of his Social Security is taxable, so his income is $77,000. His deductions total $24,580 (standard $16,600, age 65+ $2,100, senior deduction trimmed to $5,880), which leaves $52,420 of taxable income and about $6,074 of federal tax, or 7.6% of his $80,000.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysLook at where his dollars land. The pension and withdrawals fill the 12% bucket, and about $395 spills into the 22% bucket. That tells me every extra dollar Greg takes from his IRA this year costs 22 cents. A pension fills the cheap buckets first, before you touch savings.
What about a retired couple living mostly on pensions and Social Security?

Short answer: Take Dave and Karen, both over 65, with $60,000 of pensions and $40,000 of Social Security. About $34,000 of the Social Security is taxable, so their income is $94,000, deductions are $48,600, and taxable income is $45,400. Their federal tax is about $4,936, under 5% of their $100,000.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysThat's a strong position. They still have roughly $56,000 of room before their income reaches the $150,000 point where the senior deduction starts to shrink. For couples with pre-tax savings, that room is where a careful Roth conversion plan usually lives. It's not advice for your situation, though, so run your own numbers or have a professional do it.
Can withdrawals or Roth conversions reduce my senior deduction?

Short answer: Yes. Both count toward modified adjusted gross income, so large withdrawals or conversions can push you into the range where the $6,000 deduction phases out at 6 cents per dollar.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysThat phase-out creates a hidden extra tax on income in that range. If you're a senior near $75,000 single or $150,000 joint, look at the marginal cost of one more dollar, not just the bracket rate. This is where retirees overpay by guessing.
What types of income aren't taxed at the federal level?

Short answer: Gifts and inheritances you receive (the giver, not you, may owe a gift or estate tax), qualified Roth withdrawals, HSA withdrawals for qualified medical expenses, municipal bond interest, life insurance death benefits and employer-paid health insurance generally aren't federally taxable. Long-term capital gains can be tax-free in the 0% range.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysInherited IRAs and 403(b)s are the big exception: withdrawals from them are taxable. And municipal bond interest can still be taxed by your state. 'Tax-free' is rarely a complete statement, so check the exceptions before you plan around it.

Investors, families and business owners

What are the 2027 long-term capital gains tax rates?

Short answer: 0% on taxable income up to $51,050 (single and married filing separately), $102,100 (joint) and $68,350 (head of household). 15% up to $563,200 single, $633,600 joint, $598,400 head of household and $316,800 married filing separately. 20% above that.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysThese thresholds are based on taxable income, not just the gain. If you're near a line, a little planning, like timing a sale or harvesting a loss, can move gains from 15% down to 0%.
How does the 0% capital gains rate work in practice?

Short answer: A married couple with $80,000 of taxable ordinary income and $20,000 of long-term gains has $100,000 of total taxable income, under the $102,100 limit, so the $20,000 gain is taxed at 0%. With $90,000 of ordinary income and $30,000 of gains, $12,100 is taxed at 0% and $17,900 at 15%, or $2,685.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysThe gain sits on top of your ordinary income, so the 0% room is whatever's left under the limit. I like walking clients through this because the answer surprises them. Plenty of retirees and early retirees pay no tax at all on gains.
Does the 3.8% net investment income tax apply in 2027?

Short answer: It can. If your modified adjusted gross income is over $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (joint), you may owe an extra 3.8% on investment income. Those thresholds are not indexed for inflation.

Romeo Razi, CPA saysPeople see 'capital gains rates' and forget this layer. A high earner's real top rate on gains is often 23.8%, not 20%. If you're selling a business or a large position, model it before you sign.

How the IRS actually builds these numbers — step by step

Almost no consumer tax page explains the actual mechanical process behind these brackets. It isn't a committee guessing at round numbers — it's a fixed statutory formula that leaves very little discretion once the underlying data exists.

In the IRS's own words

The IRS's own release announcing the 2026 numbers states it "announced the tax year 2026 annual inflation adjustments for more than 60 tax provisions," per IR-2025-103 — the same process, on the same annual cycle, that will eventually produce the true tax-year-2027 figures this page projects.

  1. The starting point is the law, not economics. IRC § 1(f) sets the base bracket structure and requires annual inflation indexing. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed July 4, 2025, made the TCJA's seven rates (10%–37%) permanent, removing the risk of reversion to pre-2017 rates that dominated tax planning through 2025.
  2. The IRS uses the Chained CPI-U (C-CPI-U), not the headline CPI. Since the 2017 TCJA, federal tax brackets are indexed to the Chained Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers — a measure that accounts for consumers substituting between goods as relative prices shift, and which almost always runs slightly lower than the standard CPI-U most news coverage quotes.
  3. The 12-month measurement window ends in August. The relevant comparison is the average C-CPI-U for the 12 months ending August 31 of the current year versus the same period a year earlier. For the 2027 numbers, that means the calculation depends on data through August 2026 — scheduled for release by BLS on September 11, 2026.
  4. Each provision has its own rounding rule. Bracket thresholds round to the nearest $50 (or $100 for larger figures) per IRC § 1(f)(6) and related provisions — which is why confirmed IRS numbers always land on clean round figures rather than precise decimals.
  5. Treasury's Office of Associate Chief Counsel (Income Tax & Accounting) drafts the actual Revenue Procedure — the 2026 version (Rev. Proc. 2025-32) lists Michael Finn of that office as principal author. This is a technical drafting exercise applying the formula, not a policy decision at this stage — the policy decisions were already made when OBBBA set the rate structure.
  6. The Revenue Procedure is published, then sometimes amended. Rev. Proc. 2025-32 was released October 9, 2025, and then updated again on October 20, 2025, to add a new section and revise two tables — a reminder that even "final" releases can see technical corrections shortly after.
Romeo Razi, CPA

"People assume there's some group of economists sitting in a room deciding where the brackets should land. There isn't — by the time we get to bracket math, the real decisions were already made in the statute. What's left is applying a formula to a data point that doesn't exist yet. That's exactly why a projection can be quite close, but also why it can't be exact until the August CPI data is actually in hand."

What's different — and riskier — this cycle

Outcome: none of these delayed the release past the historical window. The IRS published the 2027 figures on November 5, 2026, about four weeks after last year's October 9 date.

The track record above is reassuring, but it would be dishonest to stop there. Several real, documented problems are stacked up heading into the 2027 release window that didn't exist, or existed less severely, in prior cycles.

1. Historic IRS staffing losses

The IRS's own workforce fell from approximately 103,000 employees at the start of 2025 to 74,299 by the end of July 2025 — more than a quarter of its staff — through reductions in force, retirements, and the deferred resignation program. As of mid-December 2025, nearly two-thirds of senior leadership positions remained vacant or filled by acting managers, including the commissioner and the chief counsel.

2. Unprecedented leadership turnover

By January 2026, the IRS had gone through seven acting commissioners in the first eight months of this administration. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has been serving as acting IRS Commissioner alongside his Treasury role, while Frank Bisignano holds the title of IRS CEO — an unusual dual-leadership structure with no recent precedent.

3. Two shutdowns in one fiscal year — with two very different outcomes

October 2026 update: there was no shutdown this October — Congress passed a stopgap funding bill (signed September 2, 2026) that funds the government through December 11, 2026, so the release window itself isn't exposed to a lapse. The history below is why we were watching.

The federal government shut down October 1, 2025, and didn't reopen until November 10, 2025 — 43 days, one of the longest in history. The IRS furloughed roughly half its remaining workforce for most of it. A second, brief shutdown hit again in early February 2026; this time, the IRS declined to furlough any employees at all and remained fully staffed throughout, using Inflation Reduction Act funding to do so. Despite the first, much longer shutdown, the Rev. Proc. 2025-32 release for 2026 still made its October deadline.

4. A funding fight over IRS modernization money — resolved just weeks ago

In early August 2026, a proposed stopgap funding bill would have clawed back $11.7 billion in IRS operations-support funding originally set aside under prior legislation. Senate appropriators blocked that clawback on August 3, 2026, preserving the funding. It's a reminder that IRS budget stability is still being actively fought over in Congress in real time, even as the agency works toward the October release.

5. The IRS is simultaneously implementing 100+ new tax provisions from the OBBBA

The National Taxpayer Advocate has flagged that the same short-staffed IRS handling normal operations must also update systems and guidance for over 100 tax code changes introduced by the OBBBA — a significant added burden layered on top of the routine annual inflation-adjustment process. Even in August 2026, the IRS was still issuing new OBBBA-related guidance (on overtime deductions, Trump Account contributions, and paid family leave credits, among others), confirming this work is very much ongoing.

6. Further cuts are already planned for FY2027

The IRS's own FY2027 budget justification requests shedding another roughly 4,000 staff as part of a $1.4 billion funding reduction — meaning the agency calculating the tax-year-2027 numbers will likely be smaller still than the one that calculated 2026's.

The honest tension

Every one of these six factors is real and documented. And yet the inflation-adjustment calculation has proven remarkably insulated from broader IRS chaos so far — it's a small, formulaic, low-headcount process compared to audits, phone support, or return processing, which is likely why it survived the 2025 shutdown intact, and why the February 2026 shutdown didn't touch IRS staffing at all. Our probability assessment below tries to hold both of these facts at once, rather than picking whichever one makes a better headline.
