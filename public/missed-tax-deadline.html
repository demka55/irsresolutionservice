⚠ Missed Tax Deadline — Act Now
By Romeo Razi, CPA — Former IRS Tax Examiner ·Updated October 26, 2026 ·Fact-checked against IRS primary sources ·★★★★★ 5.0 on Google (8 client reviews)

Missed the tax deadline?
Here's exactly what happens next.

Short answer: If you owe tax and missed the deadline, file your return as soon as possible. The IRS charges a failure-to-file penalty of 5% of the unpaid tax per month (max 25%), a failure-to-pay penalty of 0.5% per month, and interest at 7% a year, compounded daily (Q4 2026 rate). File more than 60 days late and a minimum penalty of $525 (or 100% of the tax, if less) kicks in.

If the IRS owes you a refund, there's no penalty — but you have 3 years to file or the refund is gone.

✓ A: IRS owes you

No penalty. Just file. You have 3 years from the original due date to claim the refund — after that the Treasury keeps it.

⚠ B: You owe, no extension

Penalties have run since April 16, 2026. The 5%/month failure-to-file penalty already hit its 25% cap in September. File now; interest and the payment penalty keep running.

⚠ C: Extension, missed Oct 15

Your filing protection ended October 15. File by December 14, 2026 to stay under the 60-day line and avoid the $525 minimum penalty.

The three charges running right now if you owe

If you owe the IRS and didn't file, three separate charges began the day after the deadline. They don't replace each other — they stack.

Failure to File
5% per month → max 25%
Failure to Pay
0.5% per month → max 25%
Interest
7% a year, compounded daily (Q4 2026)
All three apply at the same time on your unpaid balance.

One detail most guides skip: when both penalties apply in the same month, the IRS reduces the failure-to-file penalty by the failure-to-pay amount, so the combined charge is 5% a month, not 5.5%. Even one day late counts as a full month.

What it actually costs: two worked examples

Example 1 — No extension, owe $5,000, file October 30, 2026

  • Failure-to-file: capped at 22.5% (five months at 4.5%) = $1,125
  • Failure-to-pay: 7 months × 0.5% = $175, and still growing until paid
  • Interest: roughly $180 (6% for April–June, 7% since July)
  • Total added to the $5,000: about $1,480 — nearly 30% of the original tax in six and a half months

Example 2 — Filed an extension, owe $5,000, missed October 15

Here the 60-day line matters. Filing in mid-December produces two different penalties a day apart:

Filed onMonths lateFailure-to-file penalty
December 14, 20262 (within 60 days)2 × 4.5% × $5,000 = $450
December 15, 20262 (over 60 days)Minimum penalty applies = $525

On top of either figure, the failure-to-pay penalty and interest have been running on the unpaid $5,000 since April 15. If you'd paid most of your tax with the extension, the penalties shrink accordingly — they're calculated only on what remained unpaid.

What to do in the next 48 hours

File your return. Even if you don't have everything. Even if you'll need to amend it later. The moment a return is filed, the failure-to-file penalty stops. The failure-to-pay penalty and interest continue until you pay — but the 5%/month penalty stops the day the return is filed.

Romeo Razi, CPA — Missed Deadline Video

"Now is not the moment where you're trying to get the perfect haircut. Don't try to get the hard part in and the fade lined up. Just grab a clipper and shave it. File something. You can amend later. Stop that penalty now."

  1. Check whether your deadline was postponed. If you live in a federally declared disaster area, your deadline may have moved. Counties in West Virginia, Indiana, Nebraska, Mississippi, Ohio and Washington currently have filing deadlines postponed to February 1, 2027 — including for 2025 returns on extension. Check the IRS disaster relief list for your county before you assume you're late.
  2. File your return now — e-file if you can, since it's fastest. Use last year's return as a template and gather what you have. An imperfect filed return beats a perfect unfiled one; you can fix errors later with Form 1040-X.
  3. Pay what you can today. Partial payment doesn't stop any penalty, but every dollar paid shrinks the base that the failure-to-pay penalty and interest are calculated on.
  4. Set up a payment plan for the rest (options below). An approved plan generally keeps the IRS from levying while it's in effect and, if you filed on time, cuts the failure-to-pay penalty in half. Note the IRS won't approve a plan while you have unfiled returns — another reason to file first.
  5. Watch for penalty relief. With a clean three-year record, your penalties may be waived automatically or on request (see the next section). If something outside your control caused the delay — serious illness, a disaster, a death in the family — ask for reasonable-cause relief.
  6. Don't forget your state return. State deadlines and penalties run separately from the IRS. Filing your federal return doesn't stop state penalties.

Penalty relief: First Time Abate is being replaced

In July 2026 the IRS announced Automatic Exemption from Penalty (AEP), which replaces the long-standing First Time Abate (FTA) program. Under AEP, the IRS doesn't assess the failure-to-file or failure-to-pay penalty at all if you meet the three-year timely-compliance test — you no longer have to call and ask.

  • Who it covers: original returns starting with tax year 2025 (the returns due in 2026), and quarterly returns from 2026 on.
  • Who qualifies: taxpayers with no penalties (other than the estimated tax penalty) in the prior three years, or whose earlier penalties were removed for reasonable cause or IRS error.
  • Transition catch: during the changeover, some eligible taxpayers will still get a penalty notice for 2025 returns. If that's you, you can still call the IRS and request First Time Abate. AEP fully replaces FTA for returns originally due on or after January 1, 2027.
  • What it doesn't cover: the tax itself and interest. Interest is only reduced when the penalty it was charged on is removed.

What if you haven't filed for multiple years?

This is more common than you'd guess. People go years without filing — not out of malice, but because life happens, the problem feels overwhelming, and avoiding it feels easier than facing it. Romeo has helped clients who were 3, 4, even 5 years behind.

Romeo Razi, CPA — Missed Deadline Video

"In the last three weeks of tax season, I had three different people contact me. One hadn't filed for three years. One hadn't filed for five years. The other hadn't filed trust returns for four years. I was able to help all three of those people. People always talk about their highs. They never talk about their lows. This is more common than you think."

The IRS generally requires the last 6 years of returns to consider you in compliance — not every year back to the beginning. Two warnings: if you don't file, the IRS can file a substitute return for you with no deductions or credits, and any refunds older than 3 years are lost. Frequently asked questions — missed tax deadline

What happens if I miss the tax deadline?
If you owe tax, the IRS charges a failure-to-file penalty of 5% of the unpaid tax per month (maximum 25%), a failure-to-pay penalty of 0.5% per month (maximum 25%), and interest at 7% per year compounded daily (Q4 2026). When both penalties apply in the same month the combined charge is 5%. If you're owed a refund, there's no penalty, but you must file within 3 years to claim it.
I missed the October 15 extension deadline. What happens now?
The extension only protected you from the failure-to-file penalty through October 15, 2026. Starting October 16, the 5%-per-month penalty applies to any unpaid tax. File by December 14, 2026 to avoid the minimum penalty (lesser of $525 or 100% of the unpaid tax) that applies to returns more than 60 days late. The failure-to-pay penalty and interest have been running since April 15.
What is the penalty for filing taxes late if I don't owe anything?
None. Both penalties are calculated on unpaid tax, so if you owe nothing there's nothing to penalize. The only risk is losing your refund: you generally have 3 years from the original due date to claim it. For 2023 returns, that window closes April 15, 2027.
I filed an extension. Does that protect me from penalties?
Partly. A valid extension (Form 4868) protects you from the failure-to-file penalty until October 15. It never extended the time to pay — if you owed tax, the failure-to-pay penalty and interest have run since April 15 on whatever you didn't pay by then.
Can I just pay without filing?
Paying shrinks what penalties and interest are calculated on, but only filing stops the failure-to-file penalty, which is ten times larger per month than the failure-to-pay penalty. File first, then pay what you can and set up a payment plan for the rest.
Will the IRS waive my late filing penalty?
Possibly. Starting with 2025 returns, the IRS is phasing in Automatic Exemption from Penalty (AEP), which waives failure-to-file and failure-to-pay penalties automatically if you have a clean three-year record. During the transition you may still get a penalty notice — if so, call and request First Time Abate. If you don't qualify, request reasonable-cause relief.
What if I make a mistake on a rushed return?
File an amended return (Form 1040-X) to correct it. Filing an imperfect return now and amending later is usually the right strategy, because the original filing is what stops the failure-to-file penalty. Don't let perfectionism cost you months at 5% a month.
What is the estimated tax underpayment penalty?
It's a separate charge for not paying enough during the year through withholding or quarterly estimates, calculated on Form 2210 at the IRS underpayment rate (7% in Q4 2026). You generally avoid it if you paid at least 90% of this year's tax or 100% of last year's tax (110% if your prior-year AGI was over $150,000). It applies even if you filed on time.
Can I lose my passport for missing the tax deadline?
Only in serious cases. The IRS can certify "seriously delinquent" debt to the State Department when total assessed tax, penalties and interest exceed $66,000 (2026 threshold) and a lien has been filed or a levy issued. A payment plan in good standing prevents certification.
